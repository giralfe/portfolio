From gameplay code to physics solvers

I write software for games and for research: gameplay and AI systems, engines and renderers, physics solvers, and the tools around them. In a game the code has to feel right and fit in the frame. In research it has to be right, checked against something outside the code. I have done both, and the habits from each keep making the other better.

I spent four years making games at BUas. I was AI and gameplay programmer and programming lead on S.P.L.I.C.E.D., a UE5 Metroidvania released on Steam, the only programmer for the graduation quarter of Small Dragon Big Appetite, since released on Steam too, and lead programmer at a student studio. Before that I helped build two team engines that ran on PlayStation 4 and PC.

My MSc thesis at TU/e is Flatland, a 2D multibody physics engine built with Algoryx's Claude Lacoursière, where joints and contacts are exact constraints, elastic bodies are compliant ones, and one solver handles them all. Next to it I build HexForge, a C++20 engine with a CUDA XPBD solver for fluids, cloth, sand and rigid bodies, and scrye, a Monte Carlo and wave-optics simulation of an electron microscope.
